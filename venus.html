Venus

Earth's “twin” that went very, very wrong.

01 — Intro

Venus is almost the same size as Earth, which is why people call them twins. That's pretty much where the similarities stop. It's wrapped in thick clouds of sulfuric acid, and the air is mostly carbon dioxide.

All that CO₂ traps heat, so the surface sits at around 465 °C, which makes it the hottest planet even though Mercury is closer to the Sun. After the Moon, it's the brightest thing in the night sky, so you've probably seen it without realizing.

Why is Venus so hot?

Short version: a runaway greenhouse effect.

Sunlight gets through the clouds and heats the ground. The ground tries to give that heat back off as infrared, but the super thick carbon dioxide air traps it. The heat keeps piling up with nowhere to go.

The result is around 465 °C everywhere, day or night, poles or equator. That's hotter than a pizza oven.

A day longer than a year

Venus spins really slowly, and backwards.

It takes Venus 243 Earth days to spin around once, but only 225 Earth days to orbit the Sun. So one spin takes longer than a whole year.

It also spins the opposite way from Earth, which means on Venus the Sun would rise in the west and set in the east.

Has anything landed there?

Yes, but nothing lasted long.

The Soviet Union landed several Venera probes in the 1970s and 80s. The air pressure on the surface is about 90 times Earth's, like being almost a kilometer deep in the ocean.

The record holder, Venera 13, survived about 2 hours and sent back the first color photos of the surface before the heat and pressure killed it.
